Sheet 06 · F — Physical memory structure
F

Physical memory structure

how CPU · FPGA-A (Helium) · SRAM · SDRAM relateSHEET 06

All system memory lives behind FPGA-A (Helium): the CPU never sees SRAM or SDRAM directly, only virtual addresses translated by the MMU and served by the cache. The SRAM sits on the CPU's own nets, the SDRAM behind the FPGA — an asymmetry that falls out of one property of the page size.

  1. F.1
    Hierarchy: registers → SRAM cache → SDRAM (physical frames) → microSD (swap, post-v1). The SRAM is a hardware cache with tags, not a tier of allocatable frames — see L.3 for why that distinction is forced.
  2. F.2
    The key insight: with 2 KB pages, A0–A10 are never translated — the physical address is FRAME : offset, and the offset comes straight from the CPU. So both the data bus and the low address bus can be shared nets: the SRAM's address bus is the physical address bus, split at the page boundary, and Helium drives only the translated half. D0–D7 → CPU net (0 pins) · A0–A10 → CPU net (0 pins) · A11–A19 → Helium (9) · /CE, /OE, /WE (3). Thirteen pins for 1 MB, against 41 for the ×16 part it replaces.
  3. F.3
    What makes the sharing work is the half-cycle pipeline. PHI2 low: the CPU drives A0–A15 and the bank byte on D0–D7; Helium latches the bank internally — no external '573 is needed, decodes it, and — if it is not $FF — forms the 24-bit address, does the TLB lookup (~10 ns in EBR) and prepares A11–A19 and /CE. The bank decode comes first, before any translation or tag lookup, so an $FF access short-circuits straight to the I/O bus: it never evicts a hot line to read a status register, and its latency is fixed. PHI2 high: /CE asserts and the SRAM has the whole high phase (~62 ns at 8 MHz) to deliver data straight to the CPU, with no FPGA in the data path. Two dividends: ABORTB gets half a cycle of margin, since translation and permission checks finish before the rising edge; and the SDRAM leaves the critical timing path altogether.
  4. F.4
    Price of the shared nets: Helium cannot reach the SRAM on its own. Page-table walks, SDRAM→SRAM fills and the RP2040's BIOS preload all require taking the bus — RDY low to freeze the CPU, BE low to tristate it, then Helium drives A0–A10 and D0–D7 through pins it already has. No pin cost, real gateware cost. Also: three loads (CPU, Helium, SRAM) now hang off D0–D7 and A0–A10. This is the most layout-critical net on the board.
  5. F.5
    SRAM 1 MB ×8 (AS7C38096A-10TIN, TSOP-II 44, 3.3V, industrial), partitioned: 256 KB fixed and uncached — kernel, active page tables, bank $00, interrupt vectors; directly addressed, never evicted, and where the PIN PTE bit applies — plus 768 KB of cache: 384 lines of 2 KB, 4-way set associative, tags (~1.5 KB) in EBR beside the TLB. 10 ns is mandatory: /OE must be deasserted while PHI2 is low, since the bus carries the bank byte then. Direct mapping was never an option — 32,768 physical lines competing for 384 positions is 85:1. open the 256 KB may not hold the resident tables K.2 promises (→ Q9).
  6. F.6
    Fill granularity: a whole 2 KB line over the 8-bit shared bus costs ~41 µs, plus SDRAM read, plus write-back of a dirty victim — 50–100 µs with interrupts blocked, which is unacceptable. Fills therefore move 256 B sub-blocks, with valid and dirty bits per sub-block and one tag per 2 KB line: ~6 µs per fill, healthy interrupt latency, and the part of the page never touched is never transferred.
  7. F.7
    One consequence of sizing the tagged line at 2 KB: a cache line is exactly a frame, and both are 2 KB-aligned. So the "flush everything cached for frame N" operation the kernel needs before paging a frame out (→ L.16) collapses to a single tag lookup and up to eight dirty sub-block write-backs. Helium must expose that operation. It is cheap now; when the line was 256 B the same job meant sweeping eight separate lines.
  8. F.8
    SDRAM 64 MB (AS4C32M16SB-7TIN, 32M×16, 512 Mbit, TSOP-II 54) — 32,768 frames of 2 KB, one device on Helium and an identical one on Neon. 512 Mbit is where SDR silicon tops out; no 1 Gbit part exists in volume. The 256 Mbit W9825G6KH is a drop-in fallback: same footprint, same 13 address lines, one column-address bit fewer — a controller parameter, not a board change. Alternates: IS42S16320F-7TLI, IME5116SDBETG-75IA.
  9. F.9
    Off the critical path, the SDRAM controller stops being a low-latency backing store and becomes a DMA and paging engine. Substantial de-risking for bring-up: its timing no longer gates the CPU.
  10. F.10
    Architectural ceiling: 128 MB physical (16-bit frame in the PTE), deliberately not populated. A second device would double the RAM the 65816 can barely touch — at 8 MHz it sustains ~4 MB/s, so merely zeroing 64 MB takes ~16 s. A second /CS is routed to an unpopulated footprint and left there.
  11. F.11
    Bring-up requirements that come with the stall (→ L.4): SDRAM auto-refresh must be interleaved into the fill state machine; a watchdog in Helium must resume PHI2 and raise NMI or ABORT if a fill overruns, since a hung fill freezes the machine with no clock and no diagnostic path; clock gating must be glitch-free and the first pulse on resume must meet the minimum PHI2 high width; RDY is bidirectional — the CPU pulls it low on WAI — so open-drain with pull-up, never driven hard high. Free bonus: a stopped clock is literally the low-power mode.
  12. F.12
    Neon's 64 MB = chip RAM (framebuffer + audio DMA buffers), outside the CPU hierarchy and reached through the $FE window. Enough bandwidth for double buffering at 16 bpp. The PSRAM is gone from the whole board, and with it every QSPI interface.
  13. F.13
    Layout note: SRAM and SDRAM in a short comb hugging Helium — the SDRAM side runs at ~100 MHz and the shared side has three loads; neither tolerates long stubs.
