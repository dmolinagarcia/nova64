# RP2354B — embedded controller
> boot · FPGA programming · USB and HID · the ways back in

It's the laptop's "management microcontroller": it handles everything that happens before a live CPU exists, and stays on watch afterward. **The part is now a single RP2354B in QFN-80**, and that change is the subject of [D.8](sec_ai_d#d8) onward, along with the USB topology, the on-board hub and the three recovery paths that replace the one this design used to rely on.

- D.1 — Triple role: **programmer** (bitstreams, BIOS, its own firmware), **bootstrapper** (full sequence in Fig. 3), and permanent **embedded controller**. **Its 2 MB of flash is in the package**, and it is the board's only mandatory non-volatile storage.
  NOTE: **The flash moving inside the package removes a part from the always-on domain and removes a net from the board**, and it costs the recovery argument that used to sit here. The old one was that a bad write to the external W25Q16 took the whole boot with it, but `BOOTSEL` over USB answered whatever the flash held and took a UF2, so a bricked EC needed no programmer and no header. **That path is gone** — [D.9](sec_ai_d#d9) commits the USB port to host mode and it cannot be borrowed back — and it is replaced by three paths that do not involve USB at all ([D.13](sec_ai_d#d13)).
- D.2 — 9-step boot (Fig. 3): button → rails → SSPI configuration of Helium/Neon(/Argon) with CRESET_B/CDONE → SDRAM initialized by gateware → BIOS from SD to SRAM → SD handoff → RESB↑ → `$FFFC` vector.
  NOTE: **Configure Neon first, and the rest of that sequence becomes visible while it happens.** Neon's `CDONE` is the point at which the panel starts showing a correct blank screen with no register written; from there the EC switches its SPI into service-port mode ([T.46](sec_ai_t#t46)) and writes each subsequent step to the text buffer — Helium configuring, the card mounting, the BIOS loading, the CPU released. **A failure in any of those steps is then on the panel instead of being inferred from a dark one**, which is the whole argument for the service port and the reason the configuration order is a requirement rather than a preference (→ [T.53](sec_ai_t#t53), [T.64](sec_ai_t#t64)).
- D.3 — Debugging: a debug port into FPGA-A (Helium) — live read/write of system memory, a free "ICE" for the kernel. The EC does not master the bus to do it: it drives a **debug agent inside Helium** over a dedicated SPI chip select, and that block issues the accesses as a peer of the CPU port. Specified in [sheet R](sec_ai_r).
  NOTE: **That was one half of the debug story and the other half used to be missing.** The EC can halt and inspect the whole machine; nothing could halt or inspect the EC, and by [D.7](sec_ai_d#d7) its firmware holds the 10-second watchdog and the shutdown state machine — the parts whose misbehaviour looks like a hung kernel or a corrupt filesystem rather than like a firmware bug. **It is closed: `J-DBG` carries `SWCLK`, `SWDIO`, `GND` and the console UART on one 5-pin header** ([D.12](sec_ai_d#d12)), one Raspberry Pi Debug Probe services all of it over a single cable, and SWD is now the *daily* programming path rather than a concession ([D.13](sec_ai_d#d13)).
- D.4 — Console: **a UART on `J-DBG`**, and it is the EC's only console. The USB-CDC console the earlier arrangement carried is gone with the device-mode port ([D.9](sec_ai_d#d9)); the same Debug Probe that programs the EC bridges this UART to the host, so the cable count did not change. It's the terminal for the monitor (E2) and for development work throughout.
  NOTE: It is **no longer the only console, and no longer the first one.** Neon's text mode reaches the panel at [E1.7](sec_ai_p#e17), before Helium's UART exists and before the CPU runs at all, and the EC drives it over the service port with no bus in the picture ([T.64](sec_ai_t#t64)). The two are complementary rather than redundant: the serial line survives a dead panel and carries a host debugger, the screen survives a dead CPU and needs no PC attached.
- D.5 — HID host: **the native USB controller in host mode, behind an on-board hub** — USB keyboard and mouse (PS/2 and matrix removed). It powers the downstream ports on demand and translates HID → events to the kernel over the EC channel.
  NOTE: **The hub is part of the machine, not an accessory the user supplies**, and it is now an IC on the board rather than something plugged into one port ([D.10](sec_ai_d#d10)). A portable computer needs more than one socket without a dongle, and a known-good on-board hub also removes a variability source during bring-up.
  NOTE: The channel those events ride on is the half of this link still unwritten: [sheet H](sec_ai_h) names its endpoints, [sheet S](sec_ai_s) settled the power traffic, and HID has no map yet.
- D.6 — Its own pin budget nearly failed too, and was rescued the same way the FPGA's was. Straight onto GPIO the EC needed roughly 38 pins against 30 available; an **MCP23017 I2C expander** absorbed everything with no timing requirement — the three CDONE lines, the charger's STAT and PG, the fuel gauge's alert, and the enables for the 1.2V rail, the panel and the host boost — bringing it to 28 of 30.
  NOTE: The pattern had appeared twice: both Helium and the EC ran out of pins, and both were saved by moving slow signals onto a cheaper transport — the keyboard matrix onto USB HID, the enables and status lines onto I2C. **Expect it a third time**, and [sheet X](sec_ai_x) is where it happened.
  NOTE: **The expander is retired, and this is the clearest thing the part change bought.** QFN-80 gives **48 GPIO** against the 30 that forced the expander, which absorbs REV C's per-rail `EN` and `PG` ([sheet C](sec_ai_c)), the two Helium handshake pins, and the hub's reset line with room left — and it removes the question of which power domain the expander itself lives in, which was open and awkward because an expander must sit on AON to drive an enable at all while its outputs cross into the switched domain ([Q41](sec_ai_q#q41) closes on this). One constraint disappears with it: PIO-USB's requirement that `D+` and `D−` sit on two consecutive GPIOs was a `pico-pio-usb` restriction, and there is no PIO-USB any more.
- D.7 — **EC in operation, and this is no longer a sketch.** The button (short press ordered, ~4 s forced, `QON` for the hardware floor), the gauge and charger readout at 1 Hz, the telemetry push into Helium, and a shutdown that stays graceful with the OS hung — all specified in [sheet S](sec_ai_s), which also closes what used to be the EC↔kernel protocol question.
  NOTE: What the EC now owns that it did not before: **it is the only thing that can cut a rail**, and it holds the 10-second watchdog that decides when a kernel has stopped answering. That makes its firmware state machine part of the machine's data-integrity story, not just its housekeeping.

## The part — what changed, and what it deletes rather than adds.

- D.8 — **One RP2354B, QFN-80, A4 stepping**, and the three things it removes are worth more than the pin count on its own. **48 GPIO** removes the pressure that motivated both the expander and, in an earlier arrangement, a second microcontroller. **2 MB of flash in the package** removes a component from the always-on domain and the net that went with it. And a **hardware USB host controller** removes the bit-banged PIO stack from the critical path — the silicon handles hub enumeration, the `PRE` packets Low Speed devices need, and bulk transfers, none of which anybody wants to debug in software.
  NOTE: **Confirm the stepping from the package marking on the parts as received, not from the distributor's part number.** RP2354 never shipped as A2, but the marking is the only thing that settles it (→ [Q104](sec_ai_q#q104)). Every GPIO assignment must also be validated against the function-mux table before capture, which is not the same exercise as counting pins (→ [Q105](sec_ai_q#q105)).
- D.9 — **The native controller is assigned to host mode, and that assignment is one-way.** The USB-A receptacles source VBUS as a host, so attaching a PC would need an A-to-A cable with two 5 V supplies opposing each other: **`BOOTSEL` over USB is not structurally available, not merely unsupported.** Recovery is therefore restructured into three tiers that touch no USB port at all ([D.13](sec_ai_d#d13)), and the debug console is a UART ([D.4](sec_ai_d#d4)) rather than a second USB device.
  NOTE: **The USB-C connector carries power only** — VBUS and the two CC lines into the CH224K, with `D+`/`D−` unconnected. Which also settles [C.11](sec_ai_c#c11)'s warning by construction rather than by discipline: the CH224K cannot be tempted onto the data lines because there are none on that connector.
  NOTE: The alternative arrangements were examined and rejected. **Native as device with PIO as host** preserves drag-and-drop programming but puts the hub, the Low Speed `PRE` handling and the bulk transfers on the most fragile protocol path in the most fragile implementation. **Native as host with PIO as device** for a CDC console is defensible and is held as a fallback if a USB console ever proves necessary — it would *not* restore `BOOTSEL`, which needs the native controller in device mode. **A USB role mux** would restore it, at the cost of a part in the differential path, a connector and VBUS interlock logic, to recover a capability three independent tiers already cover.

## USB topology — one Full Speed bus, shared.

- D.10 — **The hub's upstream port is Full Speed, and that is specification-mandated rather than a compromise.** It gets no answer to its Hi-Speed chirp from a Full Speed host, so it reverts to Full Speed and behaves as a pure repeater with the transaction translator bypassed — exactly what happens when a USB 2.0 hub is plugged into a USB 1.1 port. Everything downstream shares 12 Mbps; a Full Speed hub is a repeater and not a switch, so downstream traffic is retransmitted to every enabled port and only the device matching the token's address answers.
  NOTE: **Low Speed devices behind a hub need the host to emit a `PRE` packet before each transaction.** It is supported in silicon here, and it is historically the most fragile path in this family's host stack. **Verify it early, with a real keyboard** (→ [Q102](sec_ai_q#q102)).
  NOTE: **The on-board hub consumes the single supported hub level, and in a portable computer a user will plug another one in.** The tier limit has to be raised and multi-level handling verified rather than assumed, and if cascading proves unreliable the fallback is stated now: **detect a downstream hub during enumeration and refuse it with a message on the console**, rather than failing silently (→ [Q103](sec_ai_q#q103)).
- D.11 — **`JP-BYPASS` puts a series jumper on `D+`/`D−` ahead of the hub with an alternate route straight to the first receptacle, and its value is entirely diagnostic.** At 12 Mbps a jumper costs nothing in signal integrity. What it buys is the difference between *"does the host stack work at all"* and *"does the hub work"* — **without it, a board that enumerates nothing tells you nothing about which of the two is at fault.** Three-pin header with a shunt, or two 0 Ω pads with one populated (→ [Q106](sec_ai_q#q106)).
- D.12 — **Silkscreen the pin order on both debug headers.** Transposing `SWCLK` and `SWDIO` during bring-up costs hours, and so does guessing at `J-QSPI`.

## Connector inventory — the EC's, not the machine's.

| Ref | Type | Function | Signals |
|---|---|---|---|
| `J-PWR` | USB-C receptacle | PD sink input | VBUS, CC1, CC2, GND. **`D+`/`D−` not connected** |
| `J-HOST1` · `J-HOST2` | USB-A receptacles | Downstream ports 1 and 2 | Switched VBUS, `D+`, `D−`, GND |
| `J-EXP` | 4-pin internal header | Downstream port 3, expansion | 5 V, `D+`, `D−`, GND |
| `J-DBG` | 5-pin header, 2.54 mm | **SWD + console UART** | `SWCLK`, `SWDIO`, GND, `UART_TX`, `UART_RX` |
| `J-QSPI` | 8-pin header | Bootrom UART boot **and** direct flash | `QSPI_SS`, `SCLK`, `SD0`–`SD3`, GND, 3V3 |
| `JP-BYPASS` | 3-pin jumper | Hub bypass ([D.11](sec_ai_d#d11)) | `D+`/`D−` routing select |
| `SW-RUN` · `SW-BOOT` · `SW-UBOOT` | Buttons or pads | Reset · BOOTSEL entry · UART boot select | `RUN`→GND · `QSPI_SS`→GND via 1 kΩ · `QSPI_SD1`→3V3 via 1 kΩ |

  NOTE: The 1 kΩ in series with `QSPI_SS` is **required**, so the button cannot fight the EC when it drives chip select during normal flash access. Place it close to the pin. Put the three buttons adjacent and document the entry sequence — hold `SW-BOOT` and `SW-UBOOT`, pulse `SW-RUN`, release — on the silkscreen beside them.

## Programming and recovery — three tiers, none of which touches a USB port.

| Tier | Path | Interface | Writes | Survives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Daily | SWD | `J-DBG` | Flash + SRAM | — |
| 1 | SWD rescue debug port | `J-DBG` | Chip reset | Hung firmware, code that reconfigures the debug pins, corrupt flash |
| 2 | Bootrom UART boot | `J-QSPI` | **SRAM only** | A missing or broken SWD probe |
| 3 | Direct QSPI to the flash die | `J-QSPI` + `RUN` low | Flash die | Everything short of physical damage |

- D.13 — **Tier 0 is SWD and it is two signals plus ground.** Flash programming works by loading a flash algorithm into SRAM which then calls the bootrom's flash routines; OpenOCD handles that transparently, and **`picotool` is not available over SWD** — it speaks its protocol over USB only, so OpenOCD is the tool for this project.
  NOTE: **Tier 1 is the reason SWD is the primary path rather than merely the convenient one.** The debug architecture exposes a **rescue debug port** alongside the two cores, and it can reset the chip even when firmware is actively hostile to debugging — a tight loop, a routine that reconfigures the debug pins, a crashed sequencer. For an EC whose firmware sequences the rails that feed the FPGAs, that matters more than usual: **a bug in the sequencer must never leave the board unreachable.**
- D.14 — **Tier 2 is the bootrom's UART boot, and its limitation is the whole reason [D.15](sec_ai_d#d15) exists.** The bootrom checks two straps at reset — `QSPI_SS` low enters BOOTSEL, `QSPI_SD1` high selects UART rather than USB — and then speaks 1 Mbaud on `QSPI_SD2` and `QSPI_SD3`. Those are flash-interface pins, not GPIO, and are not remappable; it is a different UART from the console on `J-DBG`. **It writes only to SRAM. It cannot program flash.**
  NOTE: The strap is safe because the bootrom leaves chip select output-disabled and pulled high, so the in-package flash stays deselected, is never clocked, and its data pins stay high-impedance rather than opposing the `SD1` strap.
  NOTE: **The RAM flash-writer stub is what makes tier 2 useful, and it is a real deliverable that must exist before it is needed.** Built to link entirely into SRAM, it receives the payload over that same bootrom UART and writes flash by calling the bootrom's own flash functions, with a **CRC-32 readback** to verify — and the assembly CRC-32 from [Y2.4](sec_ai_y2#y24) is reusable for it unchanged. About 200 lines. **Archive the built binary alongside every EC firmware release: a recovery tool that has to be rebuilt from source during an emergency is not a recovery tool** (→ [Q107](sec_ai_q#q107)).
- D.15 — **Tier 3 addresses the flash die directly, and it works because holding `RUN` low puts the processor's QSPI pins in high impedance.** The in-package die is a standard part that speaks single-bit SPI, so four signals — chip select, clock, and the two data lines — are enough, and all four are on `J-QSPI`. Any SPI flash programmer will do.
  NOTE: **The hazard is that those nets are simultaneously exposed on a header and connected to the in-package flash die**, so external noise on them can destabilise the running system. Keep the header stubs as short as physically possible; **route them away from the SDRAM, the backlight boost and the boost converter's switching node**; ground-guard them if there is room; and consider 33 Ω series resistors on the header side to damp reflections from the unterminated stub (→ [Q108](sec_ai_q#q108)).

## The hub IC and its power.

- D.16 — **The candidate is a four-port hub that runs standalone from pin strapping, with per-port power control and overcurrent sense**, chosen over two cheaper parts on documentation grounds: it is the only candidate whose datasheet meets the project's no-black-boxes rule, and per-port control lets the EC kill a misbehaving port without cycling the whole bus. **The cost delta is irrelevant against the schedule cost of debugging an undocumented part.** A cheaper SSOP part with gang-only power control remains defensible if hand-assembly ease dominates on the prototype board, since it is the easiest of the three to rework (→ [Q109](sec_ai_q#q109)).
  NOTE: Configuration rules that are easy to get wrong. **Strap it self-powered**, so it can declare 500 mA per downstream port — a bus-powered hub cannot. Follow the crystal's load capacitance exactly; **crystal error is a common cause of intermittent enumeration.** Strap unused downstream ports off rather than leaving them floating. **Do not populate the configuration EEPROM** — pin strapping is sufficient and removes a failure mode. And **do not release the hub from reset with an RC**: it must be powered and out of reset *before* the EC begins enumerating, so the ordering belongs in EC firmware where it is explicit.
- D.17 — **Per-port current-limit switches driven by the hub's port-power outputs, with the fault line returned to the hub**, and 120 µF of bulk capacitance downstream of each switch to absorb hot-plug transients. **Do not omit the per-port limiting: a short on a user-accessible connector must not take down the system.**
  NOTE: **The current budget does not close on a portable, and that is a policy question rather than a component one.** Typical draw is the hub's 30 mA plus a keyboard and a mouse at 50 mA each — 130 mA, 0.65 W. Worst case is three ports at 500 mA plus the hub: **1.55 A, 7.75 W**, which a 1S battery is not going to deliver. Two answers: a single aggregate current limit ahead of the hub, letting the per-port switches handle faults only; or **the EC reading each device's declared maximum during enumeration, keeping a running total, and refusing to configure a device that would exceed the budget, saying so on the console**. The second is preferable — it is the honest behaviour, it is visible to the user, and it is exactly the kind of policy an EC should own (→ [Q110](sec_ai_q#q110)).
  NOTE: `VBUS_EN` for the whole USB subsystem becomes another switched rail in the REV C sequence: **the hub and every port stay dead until the system is up** ([C.19](sec_ai_c#c19)). Verify the boost converter's output capability at the chosen aggregate limit with the battery low, at 3.4 V in, before the schematic is frozen (→ [Q111](sec_ai_q#q111)).
- D.18 — **Signal integrity, four pairs of it**: 27 Ω series resistors on the EC's `D+`/`D−` close to the pin, 90 Ω differential routing matched in length on the upstream pair and all three downstream ones, and **ESD protection on every downstream pair and its VBUS** — these are user-accessible connectors. The host-mode pull-downs are internal to the part and need no external components.

## Do not do — the list of irreversible or expensive mistakes, kept together deliberately.

- D.19 — **Do not program the OTP.** Secure boot and the bootrom-UART disable are irreversible, and programming the security bits **disables SWD permanently and removes tiers 1 and 2 at the same stroke**. The EC has no threat model that requires them.
- D.20 — **Do not route the `J-QSPI` stubs near the SDRAM, the backlight boost or the boost converter's switching node** ([D.15](sec_ai_d#d15)) · **do not release the hub from reset with an RC** ([D.16](sec_ai_d#d16)) · **do not omit per-port current limiting** ([D.17](sec_ai_d#d17)) · **do not populate the hub's configuration EEPROM**.

- [ ] U8 — **The power policy enforced.** A device exceeding the budget is refused at configuration and the refusal appears on the console.
